

In statistics, a density curve is a smooth, continuous curve that describes the overall shape of a distribution for a quantitative variable.

Think of it as a "smoothed-out" version of a histogram. Instead of showing the data as individual bars, it shows the probability of where data points fall across a range of values.

Here are the key rules that define a density curve:

1. It is always on or above the horizontal axis.
The curve never dips below the x-axis (y ≥ 0). You can never have a negative probability of data existing in a certain range.

2. The total area under the curve is exactly 1 (or 100%).
Because every data point in the distribution must fall somewhere along the x-axis, the entire area under the curve represents 100% of the data.

3. The area under the curve between two points equals the proportion of data that falls in that range.
This is the most important rule. For example:

If you want to know what percentage of values fall between 10 and 20, you calculate the area under the curve between the x-values 10 and 20.

If the area is 0.30, that means 30% of your data lies between 10 and 20.

Two Important Differences from a Histogram:

Histograms are made of blocks (bars) that change depending on how you group the data. Density curves are a smooth mathematical function that doesn't depend on grouping choices.

Histograms show the actual count (frequency) of data. Density curves show the relative frequency (proportion) because the total area is always 1.

Common Shapes of Density Curves:

Normal Curve (Bell-shaped): Symmetrical, with most data in the middle and tails tapering off equally on both sides.

Skewed Right: A long tail stretching toward the higher values (right side).

Skewed Left: A long tail stretching toward the lower values (left side).

Uniform: A flat, rectangular shape where all value ranges have roughly equal proportions.

Important Notes About Curves vs. Real Data:

A density curve is a mathematical model (an idealized picture), not the actual raw data.

Because it is smooth, it never shows exact outliers or minor bumps that might appear in real histograms. It focuses on the overall pattern.

The median of a density curve is the point that divides the area exactly in half (50% on each side). The mean is the "balance point" of the curve (like a seesaw). In a perfectly symmetrical curve, the mean and median are exactly the same.
